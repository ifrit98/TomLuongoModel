Carry-Regime Model · Version 2 · 13 August 2026

One real signal, one honest null, and the data we can't get ourselves

V2 is rebuilt on Tom's correction: Italy as the ECB's thermometer rather than the patient, France as the suspected weak link, and the 180 / 0.85 levels as the central question. The model now finds a specific, statistically strong link from yen strength to ECB fragmentation pressure — and runs out of road exactly where Tom predicted it would.

Prepared for Tom Luongo, Matt Dines, and Vince Lanci Sample 2006–2026 · 5,161 trading days · public data only

What you asked for, and what changed

"I don't think Italy is interesting here as anything other than a signal for when the ECB is intervening in the Bund market… The weak link on the continent, I suspect is France."

That reordering was the most useful thing to come out of the V1 report, and it is now the model's architecture. V1 asked a single European spread to represent the whole system. V2 separates three roles that were previously collapsed into one:

The finding: yen strength moves Italy, sterling doesn't

Across monthly data back to 2006, with standard errors robust to autocorrelation, appreciation of the yen against the euro is strongly associated with a widening of the BTP–Bund spread — the exact spread Tom watches for signs the ECB is intervening.

Why this matters for the thesis

This is the first quantitative support in the project for the chain Tom has been describing: JPY carry conditions → European funding stress → ECB response, visible in BTP–Bund. The fact that sterling shows nothing in the same equation is what elevates it above correlation-hunting — it is a channel-specific result, not a risk-on/risk-off artifact.

Two qualifications we want on the record

Direction is not established. The relationship is contemporaneous at monthly frequency. Yen strength and Italian spread widening move together; the data cannot yet say which leads, or whether both respond to a third driver. Separating that needs higher-frequency data and a cleaner event identification than public monthly series allow.

France is doing much of the work in that equation. French OAT–Bund enters the same regression at +1.83 with a z-statistic of 8.5 — France and Italy move nearly one-for-one. So the high explanatory power of the Italy equation is substantially France explaining Italy, which is interesting in its own right but means the R² should not be read as "we have modelled ECB intervention."

The honest null: France doesn't light up yet

Tom's instinct is that France is the weak link. Tested directly on public data, the French spread does not respond to the carry variables at all: R² of 0.035, and not a single coefficient significant at conventional levels. The closest is the Germany–Japan spread at p=0.11, with the wrong sign.

We do not read this as a refutation, and here is why. The hypothesis is about funding-market microstructure — repo specialness, collateral quality, bank wholesale funding, auction mechanics. The test is being run on monthly OECD benchmark yields, which cannot see any of that. A null is close to what a correct theory of an invisible mechanism would produce when tested with the wrong instrument.

What it does establish: the public-data version of the France question has been asked and answered. Further progress requires data we cannot obtain.

"Why is the ECB so scared of a drop below 0.85 and 180?"

"The big thing here is to ask why is the ECB so scared of a drop of the EUR/GBP below 0.85 and a EUR/JPY below 180?"

— Tom Luongo

We built the threshold tests around that framing: a drop below is the feared state. The model finds every historical crossing, classifies each day by regime, and compares behaviour across regimes. Here is the result, and the reason we are not yet willing to call it evidence.

The trap in this data

Comparing regimes makes it look like something dramatic happens below the thresholds: Italian spreads average 137bp in the both-below state against 76bp in the both-above state. We do not believe that comparison. It is structurally "the last nine months versus the previous twenty years" — the wide Italian spread in the below state is largely 2011–2012. Any threshold effect is inseparable from the decade it sits in. Establishing that 180 and 0.85 are genuinely defended needs a within-period test and, more importantly, evidence of the defending itself.

Which is precisely where we need Matt and Vince. Public price data can show that the ECB reacted; it cannot distinguish a defended boundary from a level that happens to coincide with trouble.

The second model: US/UK/JP and the Swiss question

"It's obvious that there is a link between the US/UK/JP carry spread. I'm also sure the CHF/SNB is involved here, since it is now a source of carry capacity."

— Tom Luongo

Both are now in the model, with one methodological warning attached to the first.

On US/UK/JP: the long-end spreads between these three are algebraically linked — US–JP is mechanically the sum of US–DE and DE–JP. So the fact that they co-move is arithmetic, not evidence, and any report claiming their correlation as confirmation is claiming nothing. We have kept them as diagnostics only. The non-trivial result in that block: yen appreciation moves the US–UK spread significantly (p=0.001), and in the opposite direction to its effect on Italy. Sterling appreciation sits right at the edge (p=0.053). A real test of the carry link needs short rates, FX forwards, and cross-currency basis, not long-end yields.

On CHF/SNB: the public rate geometry is genuinely striking — Swiss 3-month funding is cheaper than Japanese by 131 basis points on the latest common observation, with SNB policy at 0% against a BOJ guideline near 1%. That is exactly the setup Tom describes for CHF absorbing carry capacity. But the regression finds nothing: the CHF funding advantage does not respond to yen or sterling moves at any conventional significance (p=0.18 and p=0.37). Either the substitution runs through channels invisible in 3-month interbank rates — forwards and basis, most likely — or it is not happening at the frequency we can observe. We cannot currently tell which, and the distinction matters.

Method and provenance

Everything above comes from one reproducible run over 2006–2026: 27 FRED series plus CFTC positioning, Japanese Ministry of Finance flows, and US TIC data. 5,161 trading days, 245 monthly observations. All regressions use HAC-robust standard errors.

Three points of discipline worth stating explicitly, since the model is being asked to support a strong thesis:

  • Sovereign yield spreads are diagnostics, not carry. Nothing here is an executable return. Real carry needs forwards, basis, and funding costs, which is most of what the list above is asking for.
  • Price sequencing cannot establish intent. The model can show that things happened in an order consistent with a policy; it cannot show that anyone intended it. That claim is marked as not identifiable and will stay that way regardless of data.
  • Thresholds must earn their keep out of sample. 180 and 0.85 were specified in advance rather than fitted, and are tested against placebo thresholds. We would rather report that we cannot yet confirm them than fit them retroactively.

The V2 harness was independently verified before this run, and five defects were found and corrected in the process — one of which had been mis-stating the regime day-counts by a factor of fourteen. The numbers in this document are post-correction. We mention it because a previous version of the threshold table circulated internally with the wrong counts, and because it is the reason we are cautious about the regime comparison above rather than presenting it as a finding.

Where this goes next

With no new data, the productive moves are narrow: add leads and lags to the yen–Italy relationship to separate sequence from co-movement, condition it on identified ECB operations (PEPP, TPI, reinvestment skews), and rebuild the threshold test within-period to remove the era confound. With practitioner data, the France and CHF questions both become directly testable, and the covered-carry calculation — currently the model's largest hole — closes.
